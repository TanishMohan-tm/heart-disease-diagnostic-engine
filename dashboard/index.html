Patient Information

As we age, arteries naturally stiffen and cholesterol plaque builds up. Risk rises after 45 (men) and 55 (women).
For patients: Men typically have higher risk earlier in life. For clinicians: OR ~2.0 in this dataset, likely hormonally mediated.

Symptoms & Tests

For patients: "No pain" sounds good, but silent heart disease is often the most dangerous — pain nerves may be damaged. For clinicians: Asymptomatic presentation associated with diabetic neuropathy and advanced disease.
For patients: High blood pressure forces your heart to work harder, slowly damaging artery walls. For clinicians: Sustained elevated afterload increases LV mass and accelerates atherosclerosis.
For patients: Cholesterol is a waxy substance that can clog arteries like grease in a pipe. For clinicians: Total cholesterol proxy; LDL-C would be more specific but unavailable in this 1988 dataset.
For patients: Diabetes damages blood vessels over time. For clinicians: Binary threshold is crude; HbA1c would be preferred. Weak predictive power in this model (coef −0.04).
For patients: An ECG measures the electrical signals in your heart. Abnormal patterns can indicate damage or strain. For clinicians: ST-T changes suggest ischemia; LVH indicates chronic pressure overload.

Exercise Stress Test

For patients: A higher max heart rate during exercise means your heart is fit and can handle stress. This is protective. For clinicians: Inversely correlated with cardiovascular fitness and absence of flow-limiting lesions.
For patients: If your heart hurts when you exercise, it means it isn't getting enough oxygen. This is a major warning sign. For clinicians: Classic exertional angina indicates flow-limiting coronary stenosis.
For patients: This measures how much your heart "struggles" during exercise on an ECG. Higher = more concern. For clinicians: ST-segment depression reflects subendocardial ischemia during stress. >2mm is significant.
For patients: The direction of the ECG curve during peak exercise shows how blood flow changes. Downsloping is worrying. For clinicians: Downsloping ST segment during peak exercise is a hallmark of transmural ischemia.
